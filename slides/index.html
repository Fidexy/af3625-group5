Concept primer · 1 of 2

A small country takes the world price as given

Fig. 1 · the import / export rule

Left: the autarky price is above the world price, so at that world price domestic demand exceeds domestic supply — the gap is imported. Right: the opposite case, where the country exports.

  • A small country is a price taker: its own demand and supply cannot move the world price, so the world price is a horizontal line at Pw.
  • Once the country trades, domestic buyers and sellers all face Pw.
  • Autarky price P0 > Pw → the country imports; P0 < Pw → it exports.
Qimport = Qd(Pw) − Qs(Pw)

The gap between what domestic consumers want and what domestic producers supply at the world price is filled by imports.

Concept primer

45 s · Presenter A

One idea first, because every question depends on it. Country Z and country A are small countries, which means price takers: nothing they buy or sell can move the world price, so the world price is drawn as a flat line. Once they trade, domestic buyers and sellers all face that price. The rule is simple. If the price you would have without trade — the autarky price — is above the world price, you import, because foreign producers can undercut your own. If it is below, you export. And imports are just the gap between what domestic consumers want and what domestic producers will supply at the world price.

Concept primer

50 s · Presenter A

Second idea: surplus. Consumer surplus is the area under demand and above the price — the benefit consumers get over what they actually pay. Producer surplus is the area above supply and below the price. Add them and you have social surplus: the total gain the market creates. Now the two barriers we use today. A tariff is a tax on each imported unit: the domestic price becomes the world price plus the tariff, and the government collects that price gap as revenue. A quota caps the quantity: the domestic price rises until the market clears with only that many imports, and the price gap goes to whoever holds the import licences — that is quota rent. Both cost the country the same two deadweight-loss triangles.

Question 5.1 (iv)

What a 20,000-unit import quota is meant to do

1 · Restrict supply

Legal imports are capped at 20,000 TVs — far below the 100,000 that would be imported under free trade, so the quota binds.

2 · Raise the domestic price

With less total supply available, consumers bid the domestic price above the world price; domestic producers respond by increasing output.

3 · Protect domestic producers

Higher prices and larger domestic sales raise producer surplus, but consumers pay more and their surplus falls.

Intended effect, in one sentence:

By limiting imports to 20,000 TVs, the government intends to reduce the quantity of imports, raise the domestic price and domestic production, and thereby protect domestic TV producers from foreign competition.

Question 5.2 (f)

“A tariff is beneficial because the government receives revenue” — true or false?

False — for country A as a whole

GroupChange in welfare

The gains are real, but they are transfers: consumers lose more than producers and the government together receive. The $15,000 nobody gets is the deadweight loss.

DWL = the two triangles

DWL = ½ × t × Δimports
= ½ × $25 × (4,800 − 3,600)
= ½ × $25 × 1,200
DWL = $15,000
  • Production distortion (½ × 1,150 × 25 = $14,375): resources are pulled into handbag production that could have been bought abroad more cheaply.
  • Consumption distortion (½ × 50 × 25 = $625): some mutually beneficial trades no longer happen.

So the statement is true only for specific groups — the government and domestic producers — and false for the country as a whole.

The barrier is an ad valorem tariff — a percentage tax on imports. EU consumers pay more, so their surplus falls. EU producers are protected from cheaper imports, so theirs rises. The EU budget collects the duty: tariff revenue. And fewer cars are imported, so the two deadweight-loss triangles appear. The price undertaking is the interesting line: if a minimum import price replaces the duty, the price gap stays with the Chinese exporters rather than the EU — that is the quota-rent case, exactly like a voluntary export restraint. And on comparative advantage: protection keeps production in a location where costs are higher.

Conclusion

Three things to take away

1 · Both barriers raise the price

A tariff fixes the price gap (t); a quota fixes the quantity and lets the price rise to the level that clears the market with those imports.

2 · Someone always gets the gap

Tariff → the government. Quota → the licence holder (quota rent). If the rent goes abroad, the country is worse off still.

3 · Producers win, consumers lose, the country loses

The gains are transfers. What is not transferred — the two DWL triangles — is the net cost: $15,000 in Q5.2, $100,000 in Q5.3.

Protection can help specific groups, but it does not make the country as a whole better off — the question is always whose surplus changes, and by how much.
Conclusion

40 s · Presenter D

Three takeaways. First, both barriers raise the domestic price — a tariff fixes the price gap, a quota fixes the quantity and lets the price find its level. Second, someone always gets the gap: the government with a tariff, the licence holders with a quota — and if that rent goes abroad instead, the country is worse off still. And third: producers win, consumers lose, and the country as a whole loses by the two deadweight-loss triangles — fifteen thousand dollars in the handbag question, one hundred thousand in Egypt.
