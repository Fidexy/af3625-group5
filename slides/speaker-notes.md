## 3. Primer – world price

`0:45` · **Presenter A**

One idea first, because every question depends on it. Country Z and country A are small countries, which means price takers: nothing they buy or sell can move the world price, so the world price is drawn as a flat line. Once they trade, domestic buyers and sellers all face that price. The rule is simple. If the price you would have without trade — the autarky price — is above the world price, you import, because foreign producers can undercut your own. If it is below, you export. And imports are just the gap between what domestic consumers want and what domestic producers will supply at the world price.

---

## 4. Primer – surplus and barriers

`0:50` · **Presenter A**

Second idea: surplus. Consumer surplus is the area under demand and above the price — the benefit consumers get over what they actually pay. Producer surplus is the area above supply and below the price. Add them and you have social surplus: the total gain the market creates. Now the two barriers we use today. A tariff is a tax on each imported unit: the domestic price becomes the world price plus the tariff, and the government collects that price gap as revenue. A quota caps the quantity: the domestic price rises until the market clears with only that many imports, and the price gap goes to whoever holds the import licences — that is quota rent. Both cost the country the same two deadweight-loss triangles.

## 8. Q5.1 (iii) producer surplus

Part (iii) is producer surplus under free trade. It is the triangle above the domestic supply curve and below seventy-five dollars, up to the twenty thousand that domestic producers sell. ## 9. Q5.1 (iv) quota effect

`0:40` · **Presenter B**

Part (iv): the government imposes an import quota of twenty thousand televisions. The intended effect comes in three steps. It caps imports at twenty thousand, far below the hundred thousand that free trade would bring in, so the quota binds. Total supply at seventy-five dollars is now too small, so consumers bid the price up. And that higher price pulls more domestic production in. In one sentence: by limiting imports, the government intends to raise the domestic price and domestic output, and so protect domestic producers — at the expense of consumers.

## 10. Q5.1 (v) tracing S + quota

`0:55` · **Presenter B**

Part (v) is the part the question warns you about. The rule from Topic 5 is: when the domestic price is at or above the world price, supply becomes S plus quota. So the correct curve is kinked, not a straight line. Below seventy-five dollars there are no imports at all, so supply is just the domestic curve. At seventy-five dollars it jumps to the right by the quota — twenty thousand to forty thousand. Above that it is the domestic supply curve shifted right by twenty thousand, starting at forty thousand. The curve printed in the figure runs all the way down to twenty-five dollars on the price axis: it implies imports at prices where no imports are possible. That is the error they are testing.

## 11. Q5.1 (vi) quota rent

`0:40` · **Presenter B**

Part (vi), quota rent. With S plus quota, the market clears where supply meets demand: eighty thousand televisions at a hundred and twenty-five dollars. Domestic producers supply sixty thousand of those, so imports are twenty thousand — exactly the quota, which is our check. Quota rent is the price gap times the quantity imported: a hundred and twenty-five minus seventy-five is fifty dollars, times twenty thousand — one million dollars. And notice who receives it: whoever holds the import licences, the importers, not the government. That is the difference from a tariff.

## 13. Q5.2 – setup

Before the numbers, the method, because every part uses the same three moves. Without trade, the market clears domestically: set demand equal to supply. ## 16. Q5.2 (c) the tariff

`0:45` · **Presenter C**

Part (c) is the interesting one. The government wants imports cut by twenty-five per cent, so the target is four thousand eight hundred times zero point seven five — three thousand six hundred handbags. ## 18. Q5.2 (e) consumer surplus

Part (e), consumer surplus under the tariff. At six hundred and twenty-five dollars consumers buy thirty-eight thousand seven hundred and fifty handbags. The demand curve reaches the price axis where quantity demanded is zero — that is forty thousand divided by two, twenty thousand dollars. ## 19. Q5.2 (f) is the tariff beneficial?

`0:50` · **Presenter C**

Part (f), and this is the reflective one. The statement says that imposing a tariff is beneficial for country A as a whole because the government receives tariff revenue. It is false — for the country as a whole. Look at the three groups. Consumers lose nine hundred and sixty-nine thousand dollars. Domestic producers gain eight hundred and sixty-four thousand. The government collects ninety thousand. Add those up and the country is down fifteen thousand dollars — and that is exactly the deadweight loss: a half times the twenty-five dollar tariff times the twelve hundred handbags of imports that disappeared. So the statement is true for specific groups, the government and domestic producers, but the gains are transfers. A transfer cannot make the total any bigger.

## 22. Q5.3 (b) gainers and losers

`0:50` · **Presenter D**

Part (b) — who gains and who loses. Producers gain: their surplus is the trapezoid between three hundred and four hundred dollars along the supply curve, and it is worth four hundred and fifty thousand. The same conclusion as the handbags: protection moves income around, and destroys some of it.

## 24. Q5.4 – EU tariffs on Chinese EVs

`1:20` · **Presenter D**

What happened: after a year-long anti-subsidy investigation, the EU imposed definitive countervailing duties for five years, on top of the standard ten per cent. The rate depends on the company — BYD seventeen per cent, Geely eighteen point eight, SAIC thirty-five point three, Tesla’s Shanghai plant seven point eight — so some models face forty-five per cent in total. EU members were divided, Beijing opened its own investigations into EU brandy, dairy and pork, and both sides kept negotiating a price undertaking instead. Now map it onto Topic 5. The barrier is an ad valorem tariff — a percentage tax on imports. EU consumers pay more, so their surplus falls. EU producers are protected from cheaper imports, so theirs rises. The EU budget collects the duty: tariff revenue. And fewer cars are imported, so the two deadweight-loss triangles appear. The price undertaking is the interesting line: if a minimum import price replaces the duty, the price gap stays with the Chinese exporters rather than the EU — that is the quota-rent case, exactly like a voluntary export restraint. And on comparative advantage: protection keeps production in a location where costs are higher.

## 25. Conclusion

`0:40` · **Presenter D**

Three takeaways. First, both barriers raise the domestic price — a tariff fixes the price gap, a quota fixes the quantity and lets the price find its level. Second, someone always gets the gap: the government with a tariff, the licence holders with a quota — and if that rent goes abroad instead, the country is worse off still. And third: producers win, consumers lose, and the country as a whole loses by the two deadweight-loss triangles — fifteen thousand dollars in the handbag question, one hundred thousand in Egypt.
